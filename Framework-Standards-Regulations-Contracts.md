
# Frameworks, Standards, Regulations, Contracts, Policies, and Controls

## Overview

In cybersecurity and GRC (Governance, Risk, and Compliance), it's important to understand where requirements come from and how organizations address them.

Frameworks, standards, regulations, contracts, policies, and controls all play different roles in managing risk.

This exercise focuses on identifying those differences and understanding how each category applies in a real organization.

## 1. Understanding the Differences

| Category | Definition |
|---|---|
| Framework | Provides a structured approach to managing risk, governance, or business processes. |
| Standard | Establishes requirements, specifications, or guidance that organizations can follow or be assessed against. |
| Regulation / Law | Creates legally enforceable obligations when applicable. |
| Contract | Establishes agreed-upon obligations between parties. |
| Policy | Defines an organization's internal rules, expectations, and management direction. |
| Control | A measure used to reduce risk, protect assets, or meet a specific objective. |

An organization may be affected by all six categories at the same time.

For example, an organization might use the NIST Cybersecurity Framework to structure its security program, follow ISO 27001 requirements, comply with GDPR, and meet contractual security obligations.

Internally, that same organization would establish policies and implement controls to address those requirements.

### Questions to Consider

- Where does the requirement come from?
- Does it apply to the organization?
- What people, systems, and processes are in scope?
- What controls are needed?
- What evidence demonstrates compliance?

## 2. Frameworks

Frameworks provide organizations with structured methods for managing risk, improving operations, and achieving business objectives.

### Examples

**1. NIST Cybersecurity Framework (CSF) 2.0**

Helps organizations identify, assess, prioritize, and manage cybersecurity risks.

The framework includes six core functions: Govern, Identify, Protect, Detect, Respond, and Recover.

**2. NIST Risk Management Framework (RMF)**

Provides a structured process for managing security and privacy risks throughout an information system's life cycle.

**3. NIST AI Risk Management Framework (AI RMF)**

Helps organizations identify, evaluate, and manage risks associated with artificial intelligence systems.

**4. SWOT Analysis**

A strategic planning tool used to evaluate an organization's strengths, weaknesses, opportunities, and threats.

**5. Balanced Scorecard (BSC)**

A strategic management framework that connects business objectives with measurable performance across four perspectives:

- Financial
- Customer
- Internal Processes
- Learning and Growth

**6. COBIT**

An IT governance and management framework that helps organizations align technology activities with business objectives.

### Key Takeaway

Frameworks provide structure and direction. They do not automatically create legally enforceable requirements.

---

## 3. Standards

Standards establish requirements, specifications, or guidelines that organizations can adopt to improve consistency, security, and operational effectiveness.

Some standards allow organizations to pursue certification, while others establish industry security requirements.

### Examples

**1. ISO/IEC 27001:2022**

Defines requirements for establishing, implementing, maintaining, and continually improving an Information Security Management System (ISMS).

**2. ISO/IEC 42001:2023**

Defines requirements for establishing and maintaining an Artificial Intelligence Management System (AIMS).

**3. ISO 22301:2019**

Establishes requirements for a Business Continuity Management System (BCMS) to help organizations prepare for and respond to disruptions.

**4. PCI Data Security Standard (PCI DSS)**

Establishes technical and operational security requirements designed to protect payment account data.

**5. ISO/IEC 27002:2022**

Provides guidance and a reference set of information security controls organizations can use to strengthen their security programs.

### Key Takeaway

Standards provide consistent requirements or recommended practices. Depending on the standard, organizations may be assessed or certified against them.

Standards can also become mandatory through contracts, regulations, or industry obligations.

---

## 4. Regulations and Laws

Regulations and laws establish legally enforceable obligations.

Organizations must determine which legal requirements apply based on their business activities, geographic locations, and the information they process.

### Examples

**1. General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR)**

EU regulation governing the processing and protection of personal data.

**2. EU Artificial Intelligence Act (AI Act)**

Establishes legal requirements for certain artificial intelligence systems based on their risks and intended uses.

**3. Sarbanes-Oxley Act (SOX)**

U.S. federal law focused on corporate accountability, financial reporting, and internal controls.

**4. Ireland's Data Protection Act 2018**

Provides additional national requirements supporting Ireland's data protection framework alongside GDPR.

**5. EU ePrivacy Directive and National Implementing Laws**

Establishes rules governing electronic communications privacy and confidentiality.

The proposed EU ePrivacy Regulation was withdrawn in 2025 and was never adopted.

### Key Takeaway

Legal requirements depend on jurisdiction, business activities, and organizational responsibilities.

Not every law or regulation applies to every organization.

## 5. Contracts

Contracts create obligations between two or more parties.

In cybersecurity, contracts may establish requirements for data security, confidentiality, service availability, incident reporting, and vendor responsibilities.

### Examples

**1. Statement of Work (SOW)**

Defines project-specific details such as:

- Scope of work
- Deliverables
- Responsibilities
- Timelines
- Milestones
- Acceptance criteria

A SOW may be legally binding as part of a broader agreement.

**2. Vendor Agreement**

Defines the business relationship between an organization and an external supplier.

It may include pricing, confidentiality, security responsibilities, liability, and termination conditions.

**3. Service Order Agreement / Order Form**

Documents the purchase of specific services, often under an existing agreement.

**4. Service Level Agreement (SLA)**

Defines expected service performance, including uptime, response times, and resolution targets.

An SLA may be a standalone agreement or part of a larger contract.

**5. Microsoft Services Agreement**

Establishes terms and conditions for Microsoft's covered consumer products and services.

### Key Takeaway

Contracts establish agreed-upon responsibilities.

Security and compliance teams must understand which contractual obligations apply and how the organization demonstrates that it meets them.

---

## 6. Policies

Policies are internal documents that communicate management expectations and establish organizational requirements.

They help employees understand what is expected and provide direction for implementing security and operational practices.

### Examples

**1. Risk Assessment Policy**

Defines expectations for identifying, analyzing, evaluating, and documenting organizational risks.

**2. Risk Management Policy**

Establishes how an organization manages risks, including responsibilities, risk treatment, and monitoring.

**3. Vendor Due Diligence Policy**

Defines requirements for evaluating third-party vendors before and during business relationships.

**4. Bill Payment Policy**

Establishes internal requirements for reviewing, approving, and processing payments.

**5. Acceptable Use Policy (AUP)**

Defines how employees and authorized users may use company systems, networks, devices, and information.

### Key Takeaway

Policies explain what an organization expects.

Procedures and controls help put those expectations into practice.

---

## 7. Controls

Controls are safeguards or measures implemented to reduce risks, protect organizational assets, and meet security or business objectives.

Controls may be technical, administrative, or physical.

### Examples

**1. CIS Critical Security Controls**

A prioritized set of cybersecurity safeguards designed to help organizations defend against common threats.

Examples include inventory management, vulnerability management, and access control.

**2. SOX-Related Internal Controls**

Controls supporting the accuracy and reliability of financial reporting.

Examples include access reviews, segregation of duties, and approval requirements for financially significant changes.

**3. Information Security Controls**

Measures designed to protect the confidentiality, integrity, and availability of information.

Examples include encryption, multifactor authentication, and logging.

**4. Boundary Defense Controls**

Measures used to monitor and protect network boundaries.

Examples include firewall rules, network segmentation, and intrusion prevention systems.

**5. Administrative Controls**

Measures established through organizational processes and management activities.

Examples include security awareness training, access review procedures, and background screening.

### Key Takeaway

Controls are the measures organizations implement to address risks and requirements.

Simply having a control documented does not mean it is operating effectively.

Organizations need evidence that the control was implemented and is functioning as intended.

## 8. Putting Everything Together

Consider an organization that processes customer payment information.

| Category | Example | Purpose |
|---|---|---|
| Framework | NIST CSF 2.0 | Organizes cybersecurity risk management activities. |
| Standard | PCI DSS | Establishes payment account data security requirements. |
| Regulation / Law | Applicable privacy laws | Establishes legal requirements for protecting personal information. |
| Contract | Payment processing agreement | Defines obligations between the organization and its payment provider. |
| Policy | Access Control Policy | Establishes internal requirements for granting and managing access. |
| Control | Multifactor Authentication (MFA) | Helps prevent unauthorized access to systems. |

### Example: Access Management

**Risk:** An unauthorized individual gains access to systems containing sensitive information.

**Policy:** Access Control Policy.

**Control:** Require MFA for applicable accounts and systems.

**Implementation:** Configure and enforce MFA through the organization's identity management platform.

**Evidence:**
- MFA configuration settings
- Authentication logs
- User access reports
- Access review records

This example shows how an organization can move from identifying a risk to implementing controls and collecting evidence.

## 9. What I Learned

This exercise helped me understand the differences between frameworks, standards, regulations, contracts, policies, and controls.

One of the biggest takeaways is that these categories are connected, but they are not interchangeable.

A framework may help structure a cybersecurity program, while a standard establishes requirements or guidance. Regulations create legal obligations, and contracts establish responsibilities between parties.

Policies define internal expectations, while controls are used to put security requirements into practice.

I also learned that identifying a requirement is only part of the process. Understanding the scope, implementing the appropriate controls, and maintaining evidence are just as important.

This is a foundation I want to continue building on as I develop my understanding of cybersecurity governance, risk management, compliance, and technical controls.
